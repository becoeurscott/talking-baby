Complete Game Development Blueprint

PROJECT: MY LITTLE LIFE

The vision: Create a mobile game with the visual polish, character expressiveness and satisfying interactions of Talking Tom, but with a completely original human character who experiences an entire lifetime.

The player starts by raising a baby. Over time, that baby becomes a toddler, child, teenager and adult, eventually getting married, having children and leaving the childhood home.

This blueprint is designed around an indie developer or a very small team using AI-assisted development.

1. Game identity and creative direction

MY LITTLE LIFE

A LITTLE LIFE. A MILLION MEMORIES.

Genre: Virtual companion + life simulation

Platforms: Android and iOS

Engine: Unity 3D

Rendering: Universal Render Pipeline (URP)

Camera: Third-person fixed frontal camera, Talking Tom-style interaction

Business model: Free-to-play with optional cosmetics and expansion content

Target audience: Casual players, families and life-simulation enthusiasts

The core experience

There are three pillars:

* Emotional connection: Players become attached to their character.
* Interactive entertainment: Every interaction feels responsive, animated and rewarding.
* Life progression: Players are curious to discover what happens next in their character’s life.

The game should feel like a living animated world rather than a collection of menus.

Visual identity

Use these visual principles:

* Rounded, appealing character proportions.
* Large expressive eyes.
* Soft skin shading and subtle subsurface scattering.
* High-quality hair and clothing.
* Soft shadows and ambient occlusion.
* Colorful but controlled environments.
* Smooth camera transitions.
* Exaggerated but believable facial expressions.

Important: Talking Tom is the quality reference, not an asset or character template to copy. Develop original characters, models, animations, UI, sounds and branding.

2. Complete game progression

The central mechanic is a character living through one accelerated year.

I recommend 365 in-game days, with the ability to pause time, sleep through the night and accelerate routine activities.

Life stages

In-game days	Age stage	Main gameplay
1–30	Baby	Feeding, sleeping, crying, playing
31–90	Toddler	Walking, talking, exploration
91–150	Child	School, friends, learning
151–210	Teenager	Relationships, hobbies, independence
211–270	Young adult	Work, income, dating
271–330	Parent	Marriage, children, parenting
331–365	Independent adult	Moving out, new household

These are game progression stages, not realistic human development milestones.

Stage 1: Baby

The player is responsible for the baby’s daily needs.

Interactions:

* Feed the baby.
* Give it milk.
* Change diapers.
* Bathe it.
* Put it to sleep.
* Play with toys.
* Tickle its feet.
* Touch its cheeks.
* Make it laugh.
* Listen to its babbling.

The baby must have different reactions depending on its mood.

For example:

* Hungry: cries and reaches for the bottle.
* Happy: smiles and laughs.
* Sleepy: rubs eyes and yawns.
* Excited: kicks legs and waves arms.

Stage 2: Toddler

Introduce:

* Walking.
* First words.
* Running.
* Drawing.
* Toy collection.
* Learning basic words.
* Playing with parents.
* Exploring the house.

Stage 3: School years

Introduce:

* School attendance.
* Homework.
* Mini-games.
* Friendships.
* Grades.
* Sports.
* Hobbies.

The player can complete short educational mini-games to improve intelligence.

Stage 4: Teenage years

Introduce:

* Personal interests.
* Fashion.
* Social relationships.
* Part-time activities.
* Emotional decisions.
* First romantic relationships.

Stage 5: Adult life

Introduce:

* Career selection.
* Salary.
* Saving money.
* Dating.
* Buying clothes.
* Furnishing a home.
* Building relationships.

Stage 6: Marriage and children

The player can:

* Choose a partner.
* Develop a relationship.
* Get married.
* Have a baby.
* Name their child.
* Feed and raise the next generation.

Stage 7: Leaving home

The character moves into a new home, leaving the childhood household behind.

A particularly powerful ending: the player sees their original parents growing older while the character begins raising a family of their own.

This also creates the possibility of a second generation.

Important architectural principle

Use ScriptableObjects for configurable game content.

For example, a baby character should have configurable:

* Hunger decay rate.
* Sleep requirements.
* Available interactions.
* Animation references.
* Growth requirements.
* Sound effects.

That way, you can create new life stages without rewriting the entire game.

4. Character and animation system

This is the most important technical investment in the entire project.

Talking Tom feels alive because its character is constantly communicating through movement, facial expressions and reactions.

Your character needs that same level of responsiveness.

A. Character construction

Build the original baby as a complete 3D character with:

* Head and facial geometry.
* Eyes, eyelids and eyebrows.
* Mouth and tongue.
* Body and limbs.
* Hands and feet.
* Hair.
* Clothing.
* Animation skeleton.

The character should have a consistent identity throughout life. The eyes, facial proportions, skin tone and recognizable features should evolve rather than being replaced by unrelated characters.

B. Animation library

Build the animation library in layers.

Animation category	Required animations
Idle	Breathing, blinking, looking around
Facial	Smile, frown, surprise, crying
Interaction	Tickle, touch, hug, kiss
Baby	Crawl, sit, kick, sleep
Movement	Walk, run, jump, dance
Activities	Eat, drink, brush teeth, bathe
Social	Wave, laugh, talk, celebrate
Emotional	Sadness, anger, excitement

Start with approximately 15–20 high-quality animations. Expand the library as the game develops.

C. Animation architecture

Use Unity’s Animator Controller with separate animation layers.

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Idle
    Idle --> Happy: Positive interaction
    Idle --> Sad: Negative mood
    Idle --> Talking: Speech
    Idle --> Eating: Feed
    Idle --> Sleeping: Sleep
    Idle --> Playing: Play
    Happy --> Idle: Reaction finished
    Sad --> Crying: Needs unmet
    Crying --> Idle: Comfort
    Eating --> Idle: Finished
    Playing --> Happy: Fun increased
    Sleeping --> Idle: Energy restored
    Talking --> Idle: Speech finished

Crucial: Don’t let animations interrupt each other randomly. A character shouldn’t start sleeping while eating or begin walking while sitting down.

Use animation priorities, transition conditions and interaction locks.

D. Facial expression system

Use blend shapes for:

* Eye blinking.
* Eye direction.
* Eyebrow movement.
* Mouth opening.
* Smiling.
* Frowning.
* Cheek movement.
* Surprise.

Combine these with procedural blinking, eye tracking and subtle head movements.

The player should feel that the character is looking directly at them.

E. Talking system

Implement two distinct modes:

* Character-generated dialogue: pre-recorded or generated voice lines with synchronized facial animation.
* Talking interaction: the character listens to the player’s voice and responds using an appropriate voice and animation.

For the first release, use pre-recorded voice lines and simple voice reactions. Don’t start with expensive real-time AI conversation.

B. Interactive environment

Your first environment should be a bedroom.

Every object should have a purpose.

Object	Interaction
Bed	Sleep
Refrigerator	Get food
Bottle	Feed baby
Toys	Play
Mirror	Facial expressions
Wardrobe	Change clothes
Bath	Hygiene
Window	Look outside
Television	Entertainment

Create an interaction system based on raycasting or touch selection.

When the player taps an object:

* Detect the selected object.
* Check whether the character can interact.
* Move or position the character if necessary.
* Play the associated animation.
* Update the relevant needs.
* Play audio feedback.
* Return to the idle state.

C. Character autonomy

This is what makes the experience feel alive.

The character should sometimes act without being instructed.

Examples:

* Looks at the player.
* Starts playing with a toy.
* Becomes sleepy.
* Yawns.
* Looks around.
* Waves.
* Reacts to sounds.
* Moves around the room.

Use a behaviour state machine with randomized but controlled idle activities.

Do not make the character constantly perform random animations. Actions should depend on needs, mood, environment and life stage.

6. The one-year progression engine

This system deserves its own architecture.

The game should have an internal clock that runs independently of the player’s device clock.

Recommended approach:

* One real-world day equals one in-game day.
* Players can accelerate routine activities.
* Major life events happen through progression milestones.
* The game can be paused.
* The player can adjust the pace of their character’s life.

For example, the first 30 in-game days represent the baby stage.

Progression logic

public enum LifeStage
{
    Baby,
    Toddler,
    Child,
    Teenager,
    YoungAdult,
    Parent,
    IndependentAdult
}
public static class LifeProgression
{
    public static LifeStage GetStage(int day)
    {
        if (day <= 30) return LifeStage.Baby;
        if (day <= 90) return LifeStage.Toddler;
        if (day <= 150) return LifeStage.Child;
        if (day <= 210) return LifeStage.Teenager;
        if (day <= 270) return LifeStage.YoungAdult;
        if (day <= 330) return LifeStage.Parent;
        return LifeStage.IndependentAdult;
    }
}

This is an illustrative core function, not the complete progression system.

The actual system should also track:

* Current day.
* Current stage.
* Experience.
* Character attributes.
* Completed milestones.
* Relationships.
* Money.
* Inventory.
* Unlocked locations.
* Major life decisions.

Make the growth meaningful

Don’t simply change the character model when the day counter reaches 31.

Introduce:

* A growth animation.
* A short cinematic.
* New character proportions.
* New clothing.
* New animations.
* New interactions.
* New environments.
* A short narrative moment.

The player should feel that something important has happened.

7. Art production pipeline

This is where you achieve the visual quality you want without hiring a massive art team.

Recommended workflow

* Concept design
    Generate character concepts and environment references with AI image generation.
* 3D modelling
    Create the final character in Blender. Establish topology suitable for deformation.
* Texturing
    Create skin, hair, eyes and clothing materials.
* Rigging
    Build the skeleton, facial rig and blend shapes.
* Animation
    Produce custom animations in Blender or use Mixamo as a starting point for compatible body movements.
* Unity integration
    Import models, materials and animations into Unity.
* Lighting and polish
    Configure URP lighting, shadows, post-processing and camera settings.
* Mobile optimization
    Test performance on actual Android and iOS devices.

AI tools I would use

Tool	Use
ChatGPT	Game design, architecture, debugging, prompts
Blender	3D modelling and editing
Meshy	AI-assisted 3D asset creation
Tripo	3D concept-to-model experimentation
Mixamo	Initial humanoid animation
ElevenLabs	Original character voices
Unity Muse / AI coding assistants	Development assistance where available
Claude Code / Codex	C# scripts, refactoring and debugging
GitHub	Version control

AI-generated 3D models often require manual topology cleanup, rigging and material adjustments. Never assume a generated character is ready for production.

* Character: needs, attributes and personality.
* Wardrobe: clothes, accessories and customization.
* Life: progression, milestones and relationships.

Use large touch targets, clear icons and minimal text.

Camera system

The default camera should resemble Talking Tom:

* Character centered in the frame.
* Medium shot showing most of the body.
* Slight perspective.
* Smooth zoom when interacting.
* Facial close-ups during emotional reactions.
* Camera transitions for major events.

Allow a little camera movement without making the game feel like a complicated 3D adventure.

9. Sound design

Sound is a major contributor to perceived quality.

You need three sound layers:

Character sounds

* Laughing.
* Crying.
* Babbling.
* Breathing.
* Eating.
* Sleeping.
* Talking.

Environmental sounds

* Birds outside.
* Wind.
* Television.
* Toys.
* Kitchen sounds.
* School ambience.

Interface sounds

* Button taps.
* Rewards.
* Level-ups.
* Unlocking items.
* Growth transitions.

Create an original character voice identity. A baby should have a recognizable set of expressive vocalizations that gradually evolves into a more mature voice.

Avoid generating every voice line dynamically. Pre-recorded voice assets will give you better consistency, lower costs and more predictable gameplay.

10. Save system and data architecture

A player could spend months developing an attachment to their character. Losing that progress would be disastrous.

I recommend two layers:

Local save

* Saves the game automatically.
* Stores character data.
* Stores inventory and currency.
* Stores progression and milestones.
* Works offline.

Cloud save (later)

* Cross-device synchronization.
* Account recovery.
* Backup.
* Multiple device support.

Example of the save structure:

{
  "character": {
    "name": "Oliver",
    "lifeStage": "Baby",
    "currentDay": 12,
    "happiness": 85,
    "health": 95,
    "intelligence": 20,
    "social": 15,
    "energy": 70
  },
  "progression": {
    "completedMilestones": [
      "firstSmile"

    "hasChildren": false,
    "movedOut": false

    "coins": 250

This is an example data contract. In production, validate save files, version the schema and avoid trusting client-side currency values if you introduce online purchases.

12. AI-assisted development workflow

You can use AI to reduce the amount of manual programming and speed up asset production. However, you should treat AI as a development assistant, not as an autonomous game studio.

I would organize the workflow into five roles.

ChatGPT — Game director

Game design documents, feature specifications, character concepts, architecture and debugging explanations.

Blender — Art production

Character modelling, rigging, environment creation and animation editing.

Unity — Game production

Rendering, animation controllers, interaction systems, physics and builds.

Claude Code / Codex — Programming assistant

C# implementation, code review, debugging, refactoring and project organization.

GitHub — Project management

Version history, backups, branches and collaboration.

The development loop

Every feature should follow the same process:

* Ask AI to design the feature.
* Get the technical specification.
* Implement it in Unity.
* Test it in the editor.
* Test it on a real phone.
* Fix visual and gameplay problems.
* Commit the working version to GitHub.

Never let AI rewrite large parts of your project without reviewing the changes and testing the result.

14. Risks to address early

Risk	Solution
Too many character models	Build a reusable character design and rigging pipeline
Animation inconsistency	Centralized animation controller
Large mobile builds	Optimize textures, meshes and audio
Poor device performance	Test on lower-end Android devices early
Development becoming too expensive	Use pre-recorded audio and offline gameplay
Losing player progress	Automatic local saves and backups
Copyright problems	Original characters, voices, environments and branding
One-year progression feeling rushed	Offer adjustable time speed and optional extended lifespan

Also, because the game includes a baby and eventually family relationships, design its advertising, privacy and monetization carefully for younger audiences. Avoid manipulative purchases or collecting unnecessary personal data.

16. Definition of success: the first playable version

Before expanding into childhood, you should be able to demonstrate this:

* The baby looks polished.
* It blinks naturally.
* It follows the player’s touch.
* It laughs when tickled.
* It cries when hungry.
* It drinks from a bottle.
* It sleeps when tired.
* It responds to voice interactions.
* Its facial expressions transition smoothly.
* The game runs smoothly on a real phone.
* The player’s progress is saved.

If you achieve this, you have the foundation of your game.

My final strategic recommendation

There is one design decision I would make from the beginning:

The child should eventually become the parent.

Imagine this:

You spend the first part of the game raising Oliver. You teach him to walk, send him to school, help him build friendships and watch him grow.

Years later, Oliver gets married and has a baby.

You now get to experience the same parenting mechanics again, but through the next generation.

That gives the game replayability beyond its initial 365-day journey.

It could become a generational life simulator with the intimate character interactions that make Talking Tom so appealing.

What I recommend we do next

Let’s not jump into coding the entire game. Our next concrete milestone should be the character production package:

* Design the original baby character.
* Establish its exact appearance and proportions.
* Create its front, side and back references.
* Define its facial expressions.
* Prepare the 3D modelling and rigging specification.
* Create the first animation list.

Once the character is right, everything else becomes easier to build around it.
